Retirement

What is a 401(k)?

A workplace retirement account that lets eligible employees contribute part of their pay, often with potential employer contributions.

Read the guide

How it works: Contributions are generally deducted through payroll and invested according to the options available in the employer’s plan. A traditional 401(k) and Roth 401(k) receive different tax treatment.

Important considerations: Plan fees, vesting schedules, withdrawal rules, available investments, and annual contribution limits can affect the account. Rules and limits may change each year.

Start here: Review your plan documents and determine whether your employer offers a contribution match.

Retirement

What is a Roth IRA?

An individual retirement account funded with after-tax money, with qualified withdrawals generally available tax-free.

Read the guide

How it works: You contribute money that has already been taxed. Eligibility, annual limits, and qualified-withdrawal requirements are governed by federal tax rules.

Important considerations: Income limits may restrict direct contributions, and investment results are not guaranteed. An IRA is an account type—not an investment itself.

Start here: Learn the difference between the account and the investments held inside it before making decisions.

Foundations

How compound growth works

Growth can build on both the money contributed and the prior growth already accumulated over time.

Read the guide

The main drivers: Starting balance, contribution amount, time invested, and rate of return all affect a projection.

Why time matters: Longer periods give potential growth more time to compound, although actual returns vary and losses are possible.

Saving

Building an emergency fund

A dedicated cash reserve can help cover unexpected expenses without immediately relying on high-interest debt.

Read the guide

Choose a target: Start with an achievable first milestone, then work toward a reserve based on your essential expenses and personal circumstances.

Keep it accessible: Emergency savings are generally intended to be liquid and separate from everyday spending.

Build consistently: Regular automatic transfers can turn a large goal into a manageable habit.

Educational information only. Account rules, eligibility, tax treatment, and contribution limits can change. Consult the appropriate licensed or tax professional regarding your circumstances.
